# Peer-reviewed background literature and how it informs this study

## Recommended primary inspiration: Huff (1964)

**Huff, D. L. (1964).** Defining and Estimating a Trading Area. *Journal of Marketing, 28*(3), 34–38. https://doi.org/10.1177/002224296402800307

**What it contributes:** The trading-area framework links shopping destination choice to spatial access and destination attractiveness. We adapt its basic idea to four Atlanta mall-area CBGs and ask how the observed origin–destination mobility distribution varies with driving distance and neighborhood characteristics. **Our PPML is an empirical spatial-flow extension, not an exact replication of Huff's choice model.** We do not observe purchases or individual choice sets.

## Recommended methodological extension: Fotheringham (1983)

**Fotheringham, A. S. (1983).** A New Set of Spatial-Interaction Models: The Theory of Competing Destinations. *Environment and Planning A, 15*(1), 15–36. https://doi.org/10.1177/0308518X8301500103

**What it contributes:** Destination competition and spatial configuration can bias ordinary gravity-model distance parameters. This motivates comparing four destinations rather than only one and using destination fixed effects. **Fixed effects do not fully implement or solve Fotheringham's competing-destinations specification.** We present this as a limitation and extension.

## Retail mix / attraction: Brueckner (1993)

**Brueckner, J. K. (1993).** Inter-store Externalities and Space Allocation in Shopping Centers. *The Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics, 7*(1), 5–16. https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrefec/v7y1993i1p5-16.html

**What it contributes:** The mix of stores can make a shopping center more attractive through inter-store externalities. It motivates comparing the distinct retail environments of Lenox and Greenbriar. Our existing model uses mall fixed effects as a coarse proxy for time-invariant attractiveness; **it does not measure store mix quantitatively**.

## Additional spatial-interaction reference

**Fotheringham, A. S. (1984).** Spatial Flows and Spatial Patterns. *Environment and Planning A, 16*(4), 529–543. https://doi.org/10.1068/a160529

**What it contributes:** Spatial configuration and accessibility affect estimated distance decay, reinforcing the need for sensitivity checks and careful interpretation.

## Suggested presentation narrative (accurate wording)

> We began with Huff's (1964) retail trading-area framework, which suggests that the accessibility and attractiveness of shopping destinations shape where activity occurs. Fotheringham (1983) shows why competing destinations and spatial structure matter for gravity models. Inspired by these studies, we assembled 2024 neighborhood mobility, Census socioeconomic measures, and driving distances to examine four Atlanta retail destinations, with a focused Lenox–Greenbriar comparison. Our estimates are descriptive and provisional because the source geography and origin-reporting rules require validation.

**Do not say:** "We reproduced Huff's model" or "Huff proved that high-income neighborhoods prefer luxury malls." Neither is supported by this study.
